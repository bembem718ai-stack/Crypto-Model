The payer clause is something that I added to be a requirement to pass something off as a success. This idea came from what an edge essentially is, the payer clause looks at the fact that in order for an edge to be an edge and maintain itself, it needs a bounded loser. So, the payer clause makes a hypothesis look at itself and try and see who pays at the cost of this, and if it can't it's either dropped, or degraded heavily. This explains why a lot of the 256 registered tests came back without any findings, or as failures.
The hardened no payer test is an extra requirement that I added to stop having no payer identified from being a free pass. It fixes the fact that if you define a payer narrowly enough, every spot-only program in the project will have no payer, and I could write that conclusion without ever looking at the data. So, the hardened version is meant to fix this by requiring two things when you claim no payer. One being naming which forced flow you actually examined - so looking into what actually made someone lose, such liquidation. Second, a test would have to say where that money went instead.
There are three sealed programs that are currently running to collect data that isn't currently available, over the span of time, to accumulate data that won't exist, so the sealed programs have something to actually test on - and possibly find a payer. First off, there is the funding program which is supposed to be finished by around June of 2027. Then there is the positioning, which will be finished by January of 2028, and finally the OI/basis which will be done in July of 2028. All of these are registered with locked rules before their data exists, so this takes out the risk of tuning, by making it impossible by construction rather than by discipline.
The shared finding across all three sealed programs is that I applied the payer clause to all of them, and all sealed programs came back as having no payer identified, all for the same reason. The forced flow is actually real - over leveraged traders get liquidated by the margin engine, however it settles on perpetual futures, rather than the spot that my programs actually buy. Additionally it happens during the initial major fall, but my program enters a day or two later - and thus the money goes to who was actually there at the time, and it isn't my model. For the money to actually go to me, I'd have to arrive when a major dump or rise is happening, or trade perpetual futures. I can do neither.
Since the payer analysis says the money goes elsewhere, the prediction is that all three sealed programs will probably fail when they run in 2027-2028. The asymmetry is the interesting part because if any of them actually pass, it goes against the payer analysis, and it would mean that the reasoning about where the money goes was wrong - so the prediction can genuinely lose. But, if all of the three do end up failing, it doesn't confirm much, as failure is also what the nine already-closed other axes look like. So essentially, a pass kills the analysis, and a failure barely changes anything.
