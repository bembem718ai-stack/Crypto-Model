The turnover null was an issue that I encountered when testing the strategy against the placebo. In my model, the strategy was to pick the top 5 coins and recalibrate every week, and this was supposed to be replicated in the placebo. However, this is not what happened, and the placebo rebalanced every day - this caused a constant change in the strategy, and the amount of transactions it made. These transactions racked up 13.6x the trading volume, and 42 percentage points per year of trading fees. It biased things drastically toward my strategy, as the placebo was crippled by the fees, and thus these fees made the placebo look terrible - and allowed for my strategy to have free passes. I caught this by measuring the turnover on both sides before actually scoring anything, and then I proceded to fix it by shuffling the labels once across the whole window instead of re-drawing it daily.
The dead weight null was an issue where when we would test the placebo, it would go back to a certain time, and when purchasing coins, it would end up buying one that did not yet exist. This caused a lot of the placebos to report a significant drop in how much its portfolio grew, since these holdings gained nothing - and some placebos held up to 30% of their holdings in said coins - thus dragging down the placebos. This tilted the scales toward my model, as it dragged down the placebo, and thus the bar. I noted that this was uncitable rather than just deleting it entirely. The allocation program had the same problem  at around 15%, however,  I caught it before scoring because the universe check existed by then. Rotation ran before that check was invented, which is why I didn't see it. Rotation's 30% is what voided #187's Discovery pass.
While testing the model, the placebo was meant to be a random pick on a coin, and to move similar to what pure randomness would've been. However, since all coins tend to move within a similar parallel to each other, when my model would pick a coin, the placebo would also pick another - and since the crypto market generally moves together - both coins would go up. So, in turn, the coins that the placebo would pick almost always would move extremely similar to that of the model - as picking 5 coins vs 5 random coins would have similar results, since the market moves in a slight parallel. This raised the bar substantially higher, because it would require the model to have a significant numerical edge to be able to edge out the placebo that would have a higher chance of pulling similar numbers to the model just based off how the market works. I caught this when I was testing on ETF's to see if my system actually worked, and in this, the testing suggested momentum couldn't beat the placebo's bar - which contradicts years of research - and from here, I looked into the bar, and I saw it was scoring 2.5% higher than a true random, and it was bending the bar. It hid for so long because I never thought that the bar could go too high, only that it would be too low - and additionally, I trusted the bar over my actual model for testing. To fix this I measured the contamination (2.5pp/year), and addedd correlation inheritance as a permanent fourth check on every future placebo, and designed an alternative null, however it turned out to be implausible on Crypto's shortened windows, so the contamination remains a noted limitation - opposted to it just being fixed.
The shadow logger was made to run my model passively across 26 separate coins and record what it would have signaled, so that in some months time, I'd have real evidence that I couldn't get elsewhere, around 18 episodes a year instead of the 1.25 from BTC alone. There was an issue however, because in order to record a signal the logger needed the exit levels - and it was getting this from a function that computes the decision but not the exit levels at all, since this happened in a later step. So now, the log would have blank target prices. This was worsened by the fact that the code that turned logged rows into episodes skipped any row without a target, and this was all the data being produced. This was thankfully caught before anything was done, and I fixed it by computing the exit levels the same way that the live path does, as well as a test that logs a buy and checks to make sure it actually becomes an episode.
There was a rounding bug that came up where prices got rounded to 2 decimal places, which was normal for high dollar amounts, but at low priced coins it completely destroyed everything. It killed entry prices, target prices, and stop losses as they all rounded to 0.00 on coins worth less than 1 dollar per coin. As a result 185 out of 645 trades in the basket had broken P&L, across 35 of the 82 tickers, and several tickers showed a false 100% win rate that just came as a result of this error. I caught this because there were three rows generated in the table output that were impossible - a 75% win rate paired with a -1.00R and 100% win rates with no expectancy number whatsoever. There were two fixes to this, and one was to round relative price to the tickers own scale, instead of the fixed 2 decimal rounding, and make the statistics function fail hard on NaN instead of averaging around it.
Overall, there were many bugs that could've been found with testing the model, especially at the rate that I tested mine - some lowering the bar, allowing for strategies to pass much easier than they should've, and then some that raised the bar far too high, doing the exact opposite and making it substantially harder for the bar to be passed. Most of them were thankfully caught relatively early, however, some hid for too long. In the case of the correlation inheretance problem, it hid for so long due to me not checking the benchmark over the actual strategy - hindering progress greatly.
