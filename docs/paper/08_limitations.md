Correlation inheritance is essentially where when I constructed the placebo it accidentally inherited some of the skill that it was trying to protect against due to the fact that crypto coins typically move in correlation with each other. It was measured at 2.5pp/yr in equities when I tested it. It couldn't be measured in crypto because the alternative null (or the time rotation) needs time windows that are longer than 48 months, and my crypto windows are 42 and 35. So in turn some crypto bars were harder than they should've been by some amount.
The tail width caveat is the idea that since the way that the placebo's data is scored and documented, the middle and average consists of many runs, but as you get closer toward the edge and the actual bar percentile, there are naturally less instances or results there, thus there is barely any data, and the edge is essentially just a guess based off of a handful of points. This is where the fidelity axes get confused, as they can verify the middle as being well documented in the placebo, but they can't for the edges - thus creating an unmeasured wobble in the exact spot where every pass or fail decision is made. So that's why #255 being the 99.0th percentile is meaningless when the bar is 99.1667, since the bar isn't precise enough to fit that amount. This can cut both ways, passing things that are meant to fail, and failing those meant to pass.
The breadth problem is the idea that if the coins were independent, more of them would been independent bets, and I could make more successes with just having more coins - even if the signal is weak. However, upon testing this I realized that the coins are not independent, and the correlation ran between .48 and .71, so they move together. So, when coins are correlated these 26 "separate" bets become a handful of bets on crypto itself - becoming what would've been just 2 or 3 real bets. This turned out to be more of a limitation, because you can't tell whether or not the strategies failed because they were bad strategies, or the fact that there weren't enough independent bets to detect a small real edge. What I found with this is that crypto doesn't offer enough actual independent assets for a model to support cross-sectional strategies at the scale I ran the model.
One of the main problems that I faced while testing the tiers of my model, was some of them became unmeasurable. The most important example of this unmeasurability would be with the Strong Buy tier, which only fired about 4 times a year per ticker, so over 5.5 years it only produced about 18 events on BTC, 8 on ETH, and a mere 1 on SOL. So in order to compute the ex_best, you need at least 30 trades - which was 3 folds carrying 10 trades each and the tier almost never got to this benchmark. The results were never anything useful about the effectiveness of the tier, as ex_best couldn't be computed, it would just come back as unmeasurable. This happened extremely often, with 0 out of 82 tickers reaching an actual defined ex_best in the breadth tests. It went undefined in 3 out of 4 quartiles when pooled, and during the funding testing, 24 out of 30 cells had at least one undefined ex_best. The overlays also furthered this issue by applying a filter to already sparse signals and tiers such as this. This was a major limitation to the project as it prohibited me from actually gaining evidence to support it as a signal, because even if it did only fire 4 times out of the year, 4 successful trades with edges could've been a great benefit. However, there was no evidence to measure since the tier fired so little.
There were some structural rules that I had set while testing, I made every decision myself, and there was nobody checking my judgement. I would almost only check on crypto, with the exception of the equities testing - but this was only to check if the model actually worked, not to find an edge. There was only one cost assumption, and it was that fees are fixed at 2+2 basis points per side, and I never modeled slippage or price impact - which was probably optimistic on thin coins. There was also only one data source, and it was Binance.US, which provided all pricing data.
The bottom line of the outcomes of this testing is that nothing cleared an honest, but slightly imperfect bar, and the imperfections are documented. These imperfections change the bars, with some bars being made too hard by things like the issue with the placebo inheriting the problems it tried to get rid of, or too easy with it holding coins that didn't exist.
